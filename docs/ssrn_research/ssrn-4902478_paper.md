ABSTRACT
ln previous papers regarding market microstructure and price information in the stock market, the VAR
model (vector autoregression) has been frequently mentioned as a benchmark, and there are various
dlscusslons about the pros and cons of this model. Here, we would like to extend the usage of this model
to the cryptocurrency market and see how VAR performs in a new setting. Our result shotazs that although
VAR might yield some gaps in terms of price prediction, it still catches the trend with a fair amount of
accuracy and could still serve as a potential benchmark model.
Keywords: Algorithmic Trading, Machine Learning, Cryptocurrency, Bitcoin, Level ll Order Book
1. INTRODUCTION AND DATA
The Crypto Level ll order book provides detailed market data and information (S. Aleti and B. Mizrach,
2021)beyond the basic information available in a Level lorder book (SN. Apergis, D. Koutmos, and J. E.
Payne, 2021). For example, the Level ll order book shows the depth of the market, revealing the quantity
of buy and sell orders at different price levels. This includes not just the best bid and ask prices (as seen in
Level l), but also additional orders lined up behind the best prices (SJ. Doering, M. Fairbank, and S.
Markose, 2017). Traders can gauge market sentiment by analyzing the distribution and volume of buy and
sell orders (J. Doyne Farmer 5, L. Gillemot, F. Lillo, S. Mike, and A. Sen, 2004). For instance, a large
number of sell orders at a particular price level might indicate potential resistance (S. Haryanto, A. Subroto,
and lt/. Ulpah, 2020), while a concentration of buy orders could signify support (M. Kearns and Y.
Nevmyvaka , 2013).
We wrote a program in q/kdb+ to conduct data collection and processing. We concentrated on Bitcoin (sym:
"X:SXBTUSD@FTX") and selected 1st Aug, 2021 for our analysis. We then use the Q programming
language to further create our four major features for our VAR model training. They are named as: "bid",
"bsrze", "ask" and "asize" respectively.
The "bid" variables refer to the top ten largest bid prices at a given timestamp, and the "bsize" variables
represent the corresponding order sizes. The "ask" variable refers to the top 10 largest ask prices at a given
timestamp, and the "asize" variables represent the corresponding order sizes. After suitable processing,
we finally get a dataset with 159109 rows and 40 columns (features). The reason why we have 40 features
is that we have 10 entries for each of the 4 major features and we decide to treat each one of them as an
individual feature.
But to reduce the number of features, as proposed in (P. Lipinski and A. Brabazon, 2014), instead of directly
using the top 10 bids, top 10 asks and their corresponding sizes as the features (T. Kuremoto, S. Kimura,
K. Kobayashi, and M. Obayashi, 2014), what we will do is construct bid/ask blocks based on their relative
distance (A. Kwan, R. Philip, and A. Shkilko, 2021)from the best bid and best ask.
First, we define something called a half-spread (h), which as the name suggests is half the bid-ask spread
(G. Pieters, 2020).
Let B and A be the best bid and ask respectively (A. Yoshihara, K. Fujikawa, K. Seki, and K. Uehara, 2014).
So we define the nth bid-block as including all bids whose values lie between (B-(n-1) h, B-nh) (J. Puddick,

2. METHODS
VAR, or Vector Autoregression, is a statistical model used in econometrics and time series analysis. lt's a
type of model that captures the linear interdependencies among multiple time series.
Basic form of a VAR model:
A VAR model with p lags is written as:
Yt = c + AlYt_t + AzYt_z + ... + ArYr_, * sr,where:
Y, is a vector of endogenous variables at time t,
c is a constant vector (intercept),
A1,42, ... , Ap are coefficient matrices,
e, is a vector of error terms.
One fascinating point of VAR is that it models multiple time series simultaneously. lt's particularly useful
when you want to understand and predict the behavior of several interrelated time series data, which is
exactly what we are doing in this paper (the 22 features correlate with each other).
2.1. Preprocessing
As we have a total of 159,109 rows of data, we decided to split the whole dataset into 10 batches, each
with a size of 15,000 (14980 for training and next 20 for testing). Also, for the VAR model to work, we must
ensure that each of lhe 22 time-series is stationary. To check this, we run the ADF test (the adfuller function
in python) on each of the series and perform ditferencing on the series that aren't stationary repeatedly
until they are stationary. Our result shows that among lhe 22 variables, all features except for the best bid
and the best ask are not stationary and they typically need one level of differencing.
2.2. Model Training and Prediction:
After the preprocessing process, we start to apply the VAR model (using statsmodels.tsa.api.VARfl
function) on the data. We decided to train the VAR model on different batches of data (from batch 1 to
batch 10), and then use the data at the subsequent20 timestamps as test set to validate the quality of
prediction. The metric we will be comparing between the forecast and actual data are bid prices, ask prices

4. CONCLUSION
Although the VAR model prediction might generate gaps between the forecast and the actual value, it's still
useful in terms of catching the price trend of bid prices, ask prices and mid prices in the high-frequency
cryptocurrency market (with accuracy of around 50% in our case). This fact partly explains why VAR models
were used in previous paper as benchmark model. ln the future, other models that have previously been
popular in the stock market (e.9. XGBoost, ARltt/A and CNN) can also be extended and examined in the
cryptocurrency market, which is much more volatile than the stock market. Furlher analyses also need be
done regarding the market impact of the trading strategies derived from the model.
